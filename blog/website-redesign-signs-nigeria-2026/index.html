Does your business need a website redesign?

Nine practical signs to help you decide whether your current website needs a focused fix, a broader redesign, a rebuild, or simply a closer look.

A website does not need a redesign simply because it is a few years old. Some older websites still explain the business clearly, work well on phones, bring in useful enquiries, and remain easy to update. Other websites look modern but make it hard for visitors to find information, trust the business, or take the next step.

The question is not, "Does my website look old?" The better question is, "Is this website still helping the business do its job?"

Quick answer: Start with the problem, not the design. A weak service page, broken form, slow image, or outdated message may need a focused fix. When several core problems affect mobile usability, content, navigation, trust, and the path to enquiry, a broader redesign may be the right next step.

Start with diagnosis, not design

A redesign is a business decision before it is a design decision. If one page has outdated copy, you may need a content update. If a contact form fails on mobile, you may need a targeted technical fix. If visitors cannot understand what the business offers, find the right service, or make an enquiry across several pages, the website may need a broader redesign.

Sometimes the problem is not the website at all. Low enquiries can come from weak traffic quality, an unclear offer, pricing that does not fit the audience, slow sales follow-up, or a campaign that attracts the wrong people. Before changing everything, look for the actual source of the problem.

1. The website no longer reflects the business

Your business may have changed since the website was first launched. You may offer new services. Your ideal customers may be different. Your team, locations, pricing, products, or positioning may have changed. Perhaps your work has improved, but the website still shows old projects and vague descriptions.

What to check

Read your homepage, service pages, and About page as if you were a new customer. Can you tell what the business does, who it is for, why someone should trust it, and what they should do next?

What the evidence may mean

If the structure still works but the words, images, proof, or service details are out of date, a focused content update may be enough. A redesign is more likely to help when the business has changed so much that the navigation, page structure, and customer journey no longer make sense.

2. The mobile experience is frustrating

Many visitors will meet your business on a phone before they ever see the desktop version. If the site forces people to pinch and zoom, hides important information, has tiny buttons, broken menus, awkward forms, or content that runs off the screen, it creates unnecessary friction.

Google uses the mobile version of a page for indexing and ranking, so mobile issues can affect both visitors and search visibility. Google's mobile-first indexing guidance explains why the mobile experience matters.

What to check

Use your own phone, not only a desktop browser window. Test the homepage, an important service or product page, the navigation menu, and your contact, booking, WhatsApp, or checkout route. Then ask someone unfamiliar with the business to do the same.

What the evidence may mean

If one form, one menu, or a few layouts are broken, responsive fixes may solve the issue. If the core templates were never designed for mobile and break across the site, redesigning those templates may be more sensible than patching every page separately.

3. Visitors struggle to find important information

A business website should make the next useful piece of information easy to find. If visitors regularly ask questions that the website should answer, or staff keep sending direct links to hidden pages, the structure may be working against the business.

This often appears as unclear service names, important details buried several clicks deep, navigation that does not match the way customers think, or a homepage that says a lot without explaining anything clearly.

What to check

Ask a person who does not know the business to find a service, a price range, an example of your work, or a way to make an enquiry. Where they hesitate is usually more useful than whether they say they like the design.

What the evidence may mean

Better navigation labels, clearer headings, stronger internal links, and improved copy can solve isolated problems. If the full page hierarchy is confusing, key services have no useful landing pages, or visitors cannot move naturally from one page to the next, the site may need an information architecture redesign.

4. The route to enquiry is unclear

A visitor may be interested in your business and still leave without contacting you. They may not know whether to call, send a WhatsApp message, request a quote, book an appointment, or complete a form. Sometimes the contact route is technically present but appears only in the footer. Sometimes the call to action is too vague to make the next step feel worthwhile.

What to check

Test the most important action on every major page. Can a visitor find a clear next step, use it easily on a phone, understand what happens after clicking, and complete the action without an error?

Important caveat

Low enquiries do not automatically mean the website needs a redesign. Look at the traffic source, the offer, the audience, response time, and sales process too. A website can be clear and still receive weak enquiries if a campaign brings the wrong people or the offer itself is not convincing.

5. The website does not establish enough trust

People use a website to decide whether a business looks credible enough to contact. Trust does not come from adding more animation or using a fashionable colour palette. It comes from clarity, consistency, accurate information, useful proof, and a website that feels maintained.

Review the site for old contact information, broken images or links, outdated team details, weak service explanations, missing work examples, inconsistent branding, or anything that makes a serious customer pause.

What the evidence may mean

New copy, stronger project examples, better photography, updated contact details, and clearer proof can make a large difference without replacing the platform. If the trust problem is built into the entire experience, such as poor page templates, inconsistent navigation, and weak content structure, the site may need a broader reset.

6. Important pages are slow, unstable, or unreliable

A slow or broken page can undo good design. Large images, unnecessary scripts, old plugins, unstable layouts, broken forms, failed payment links, and unreliable booking tools all affect the experience. Visitors may not know why the site feels difficult. They simply leave or delay taking action.

Core Web Vitals are useful diagnostic measures. They cover loading, responsiveness, and visual stability. Google recommends aiming for LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP below 200 milliseconds, and CLS below 0.1.

What to check

Run important pages through PageSpeed Insights, review Search Console's Core Web Vitals report, test forms and payments, and open the site on mobile data as well as fast Wi-Fi.

What the evidence may mean

Image optimisation, script cleanup, hosting improvements, and technical maintenance may resolve the issue. A larger change may be necessary when the platform is outdated, the codebase is difficult to maintain, or important functionality fails repeatedly.

7. The website does not support search visibility

A redesign will not automatically make a website rank higher. Search visibility depends on relevance, useful content, technical accessibility, competition, authority, and other factors. But a website can make visibility harder when important pages are missing, titles are unclear, content is thin, internal links are weak, or previous changes have damaged useful URLs.

What to check

Use Search Console to review important pages, impressions and clicks, search queries, indexing, and mobile or page-experience issues. Check whether your services have dedicated pages and whether visitors and search engines can reach them through normal links.

What the evidence may mean

Technical SEO fixes, refreshed content, improved page titles, better internal links, and stronger service pages may be enough. Redesign becomes more relevant when the templates, navigation, content structure, or platform make good SEO work difficult.

If URLs must change, map each old page to the closest relevant new page, use permanent redirects, update internal links and canonical URLs, and monitor the change in Search Console. Google's site-move guidance explains the process in detail.

8. Routine updates are unnecessarily difficult

A website should not become a problem every time the business changes. If a price update, new service, team change, product launch, or image replacement requires chasing an unavailable developer, the business does not have enough control over its own website.

What to check

Ask who controls the domain and hosting account, who can access the site, whether the business can update basic content, whether there is documentation or a handover process, and whether the platform is still supported.

What the evidence may mean

You may only need better access, clearer handover, content training, or a maintenance arrangement. If the site depends on unsupported tools, unavailable developers, or a system that cannot support safe updates, rebuilding on a more maintainable foundation may be the better decision.

9. The website cannot support how the business now operates

Some businesses outgrow their websites. A property business may need a better listing structure and enquiry flow. An online store may need product categories, payment support, delivery rules, and reliable order handling. A growing company may need better service pages, case studies, a resource area, or integrations with existing business tools.

What to check

Write down the process that currently happens outside the website. Do staff manually answer the same questions? Are leads copied from WhatsApp into a spreadsheet? Do customers struggle to find products or request an inspection? Are important internal processes relying on tools that do not connect?

What the evidence may mean

A redesign can make sense when the existing site can support new page structures and improved journeys. A rebuild may make more sense when the business needs new functionality, a different platform, better integrations, user accounts, or more reliable data handling.

Targeted fix, redesign, rebuild, or investigate first?

You do not need to choose the largest project to make progress.

  • Choose an update when the website structure works but the information, imagery, or message is weak or outdated.
  • Choose a targeted improvement when you can identify a specific issue with mobile usability, speed, forms, navigation, or content.
  • Choose a redesign when several important pages and customer journeys need to be rethought together.
  • Choose a rebuild when the current platform cannot safely support the business or its future requirements.
  • Investigate first when the evidence is unclear and the problem may sit outside the website.

Run a five-step website self-audit

  1. Test your highest-value pages on a real phone. Open your homepage, most important service or product page, and contact route. Check the text, navigation, buttons, images, and forms.
  2. Complete your own customer journey. Try to contact the business, request a quote, make a booking, buy a product, or ask for an inspection. Do not stop at clicking the button.
  3. Review Search Console. Check which pages receive impressions and clicks, whether important pages are indexed, and whether Google reports mobile or page-experience issues.
  4. Review Analytics with context. Look at engagement, devices, traffic sources, and the pages people visit before leaving. Analytics can point to a problem, but it cannot explain every reason behind it.
  5. Ask the people who use the site. Ask staff where customers get confused and ask customers what they struggled to find. Their answers can reveal problems that reports cannot show.

What to protect before a redesign

A redesign should improve the website without discarding what already works. Before work begins, make an inventory of high-performing pages, important URLs, existing forms, enquiry routes, booking or payment flows, Analytics and Search Console access, domain and hosting ownership, useful content, and third-party integrations.

Common website redesign mistakes

The most avoidable redesign mistakes usually happen before visual design begins. Starting with colours and layouts before identifying the business problem can produce a website that looks newer but still fails to guide visitors.

Changing every URL without a redirect plan can remove useful search history. Removing old content without checking whether it brings relevant traffic can throw away valuable information. Launching without testing forms, phone links, WhatsApp links, booking routes, or checkout can block the business from receiving the enquiries it wanted the redesign to improve.

A good redesign is a clearer, more useful version of the business website your customers need now.

Decide what your website needs next

If the website still supports the business, a focused update may be enough. If several core problems are connected, such as weak mobile usability, unclear service pages, poor enquiry paths, slow loading, and difficult updates, a redesign may be the right next step.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my website needs a redesign?

Look for connected problems rather than website age alone. If the site no longer reflects the business, works poorly on mobile, hides important information, makes enquiries difficult, and is hard to update, a redesign may be worth considering.

Can I improve my website without rebuilding it?

Yes. Content updates, mobile fixes, speed optimisation, clearer calls to action, and better navigation can solve isolated problems. A rebuild is more appropriate when the platform itself is limiting the business.

Will a website redesign improve Google rankings?

Not automatically. A redesign can improve technical foundations, page structure, mobile usability, and content quality, but rankings depend on many factors. Protect useful URLs and content during any redesign.

What should I prepare before hiring someone to redesign my website?

Prepare your business goals, important pages, known problems, brand assets, existing content, analytics and Search Console access, domain and hosting details, and a list of forms or integrations that must keep working.
